## One currency, two markets

The renminbi trades in two places:

| | **CNY** (在岸) | **CNH** (离岸) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Mainland China | Hong Kong, Singapore, London and other offshore centres |
| Price | Managed: daily fixing, with a permitted band | Set by supply and demand offshore |
| Who holds it | Anyone with a mainland account | Anyone with an offshore renminbi account |
| Convertibility | Controlled, with purpose requirements | Freely convertible offshore |

They are the same currency in law and different in practice. The spread between USD/CNY and USD/CNH is
a market measure of how tightly the border is holding.

## Why the prices differ

When money wants to leave China faster than the current rules allow, the offshore price weakens first —
CNH is where the pressure shows. That is why USD/CNH can trade above USD/CNY for weeks, and why the
gap widens around policy announcements.

For a resident, the gap is mostly academic. For someone with money on both sides, it is a real
question: converting at the onshore rate and then seeing the offshore rate is a loss that never
appears on a statement.

## Which one is your money

- **Salary paid into a mainland bank account** is CNY.
- **A Hong Kong account holding renminbi** is CNH.
- **A mainland account holding US dollars** is neither — it is foreign currency onshore, and
  converting it to renminbi is an FX transaction with a purpose requirement.

The distinction matters because moving between the two is not a currency conversion, it is a
**cross-border transfer**, and that is where the rules live. Converting CNH to CNY offshore and
sending it in is still an inflow, and inflows need a permitted purpose.

## The arbitrage that does not exist for you

Professionally, the gap between CNY and CNH is a trading opportunity for banks and qualified
institutions. For an individual it is not:

- You cannot freely convert CNY to CNH beyond the current-account rules.
- Structuring transfers to exploit the gap is treated as circumvention, not cleverness.
- The spread is usually smaller than the round-trip cost once fees and settlement are counted.

## What to watch

- **The fixing**, published each morning: the reference point for onshore pricing.
- **The CNH spread**, which tells you which way pressure is running.
- **Your own transaction**, where the rate you get includes the bank's spread — often several times
  the difference you were trying to capture.
