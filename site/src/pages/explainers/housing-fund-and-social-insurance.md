## Two different things, often confused

Foreign employees in China meet both of these on the payslip, and they behave differently:

| | 住房公积金 (housing fund) | 社会保险 (social insurance) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paid by | You and your employer, both | You and your employer, both |
| Goes to | **Your own account**, in your name | Pooled funds, plus a personal account for pension and medical |
| Rate | 1.5% a year on the balance | Not a return; it buys entitlements |
| Can you withdraw it | For housing, or on leaving permanently | Personal account balance on leaving; pensions only after 15 years |

Neither is an investment. Both are money you cannot spend today.

## The housing fund

A deduction from your salary, matched by your employer, credited to an account in your name. The
balance earns a fixed rate set by policy — around **1.5% a year**, well below a deposit — so its value
is not the interest, it is the employer's matching contribution.

It comes out in three situations:

1. **Buying or building housing** — deposit, or mortgage repayment.
2. **Renting**, under the city's rules, often with a cap.
3. **Leaving China permanently** — the whole balance, filed with proof of departure.

Not every city enrols foreign employees, and the rules are set locally. Whether you are contributing
is worth checking, because the contribution is a real part of your compensation: an employer that
skips it is paying you less than the offer says.

## Social insurance

Foreign employees with a work permit are generally required to participate, under a rule that has
applied since 2011. The contributions split into pensions, medical, unemployment, work injury and
maternity, and the personal pension account is yours on paper.

Two things matter for planning:

- **You usually need 15 years of contributions to draw a pension.** Most foreign employees do not
  reach it, which makes the personal account balance — not the pension — the thing you will actually
  see again.
- **A few countries have bilateral social-security agreements** that can exempt their nationals from
  part of the scheme. If your country has one, you may be paying into two systems for nothing. Ask
  before you assume.

## Getting the balances back

On leaving China permanently:

1. **Housing fund** — file for withdrawal with proof of departure. Paid to your Chinese bank account,
   usually within weeks.
2. **Social insurance personal account** — the pension and medical personal balances can be withdrawn
   in one go when the relationship ends.
3. **Employer's matching contributions** — the housing fund share is yours; the social insurance
   pooled share is not.

The gap between those three lines is where most of the money sits, and it is why the housing fund is
worth tracking even though it is small.

## What this is not

It is not tax planning, and it is not a way to move money offshore. Both balances leave through the
same bank account and the same remittance rules as any other money you earned here. See
[leaving China](/playbooks/leaving-china) for the order of operations.
