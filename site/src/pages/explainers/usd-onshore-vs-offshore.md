## Yes, you can hold USD here

A Chinese bank will open a foreign-currency account for a foreign resident with a residence permit,
and it will take a USD deposit. The currencies are limited — usually USD, EUR, HKD, JPY, sometimes GBP
and AUD — the rates are unexciting, and the tenors run from one month to about two years.

Nothing about that is a loophole, and nothing about it is hidden. It is simply a deposit denominated in
another currency.

## Where the currency has to come from

There are exactly two legal sources, and both are worth understanding:

1. **It arrived as foreign currency.** An inbound remittance from your account abroad, with a purpose
   the receiving bank accepts. This is the [inbound route](/playbooks/inbound-funding).
2. **You bought it.** A purchase of FX (购汇) within the annual facilitation quota of USD 50,000
   equivalent, for a permitted purpose. The quota is the same one described in
   [the USD 50,000 explainer](/explainers/usd-50k-quota).

What does not exist: turning RMB into USD without a purpose, turning USD into RMB without it counting,
or moving the balance to an offshore account as a favour. The deposit is inside the account, and the
account is inside the reporting.

## The spread, which is the actual cost

A foreign-currency deposit has no fee and no drama. The cost sits in the two conversions around it:
buying the currency and later selling it back. That spread is small per transaction and not small per
year, and it is the reason "just hold dollars" is a decision about a currency view rather than a
savings technique.

The bank's bid-offer spread is also not the same as the interbank rate you see quoted. Ask for the
counter rate for the amount you actually have, not the rate on the app's front page.

## Getting it out

Withdrawing USD as cash is capped and larger amounts need a declaration. Moving it back abroad is a
cross-border remittance, which needs a purpose and documents — the deposit does not make the money
"already outside".

Converting it back to RMB is a settlement (结汇) that counts against the same annual facilitation
quota. If you spent the year's quota buying the currency, you have the quota to convert back only
because the quota is annual and resets — not because the two directions are independent.

## When it makes sense, honestly

- You already hold foreign currency onshore and will spend it on something priced in that currency.
- You are staying in China for a defined period and want a currency balance without opening an
  offshore account.
- You are waiting to convert at a moment you have chosen rather than the one the payroll gave you.

When it does not: as a yield strategy, as a way to keep money outside the controls, or as a substitute
for an offshore account you are not eligible to open. Those are three different wishes, and this
channel serves none of them.
