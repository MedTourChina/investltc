## The default and the gap

When you receive dividends, interest or royalties from a Chinese payer, the default treatment for a
non-resident is a withholding at the statutory rate — broadly 20% on the taxable income, commonly
applied to dividends and interest at 10% after the reduced-rate rules.

Most of China's double-tax agreements reduce that further, often to **5% or 10% on dividends** and
similar amounts on interest. The reduction is not automatic. It is claimed.

## Why it does not happen by itself

The payer — bank, broker, fund or company — has to be satisfied that you are a tax resident of the
treaty partner, not merely a passport holder. That satisfaction comes from a document:

> A **certificate of tax residency (税收居民身份证明)** issued by the tax authority of your home
> country in the current year.

Not your passport, not your visa, not your ID card. A certificate from a tax office, usually
obtainable online, that says where you are resident for tax purposes. Some authorities issue it only
on request and the request takes weeks, so start early.

## The order to do things in

1. **Decide which country you are resident in.** If you have just moved to China and spent
   [enough days here](/explainers/tax-residency-183-days), the treaty may no longer apply to you at
   all — you are a Chinese resident and domestic rules do the work.
2. **Get the certificate** from your home tax authority for the current year.
3. **Give it to the payer** before or at the payment date, with the treaty article you are relying on.
4. **Keep the acknowledgement.** If the payer withholds at the default rate and refunds later, or
   refuses, you need the paper trail in both countries.
5. **Claim the difference** where the payer does not apply the treaty — usually through the Chinese
   tax authority, and separately as a credit at home.

## Where it commonly goes wrong

- **The payer applies the default and says nothing.** The reduced rate is a benefit you must mention.
  Silence at the counter is expensive.
- **The certificate is for last year.** Payers check the year; a stale certificate is refused.
- **The treaty says 5%, the payer withholds 10%.** The 5% bracket often requires a minimum shareholding
  (commonly 25%) and beneficial-ownership evidence. A retail holding rarely qualifies.
- **Your home country taxes it anyway.** The treaty allocates taxing rights; it does not erase your
  home-country obligation. The credit mechanism is what stops the double tax, and the credit needs the
  Chinese withholding certificate.

## What this page deliberately does not do

It does not list rates by nationality. Treaty texts change, the article numbers differ, and a table
printed here would be wrong for someone within a year. Read the dividend and interest articles of the
specific treaty — they are published, short, and numbered in a standard way — or ask the payer which
article they apply.
